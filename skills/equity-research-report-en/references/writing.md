# Writing for the reader

## Design the argument before formatting

Readers need to understand how the company earns, why the forecast looks as it does, and what valuation differences imply. Draft a one-page argument outline: question, judgment, selected evidence, and connection to the next section. Remove sections whose only purpose is recording another retrieved document.

Put economic explanations, key comparisons, forecasts, the value bridge, sensitivity, and judgments in the report. Put necessary statements, methodology details, and a concise source index in appendices. Put field-level evidence, full grids, conflicts, checks, failures, version changes, and execution logs in working papers. Rigorous research does not require reproducing all working papers in the narrative.

On revision, rewrite the section's final argument rather than appending “additional evidence” and “further qualifications.” Preserve earlier versions separately; do not leave old and revised conclusions alive in the same narrative.

## Paragraphs that reach judgments

Develop each paragraph around an economic question: state the judgment, select evidence explaining the mechanism, and connect it to business, forecasts, cash, or value. Adapt the sequence to the argument; do not force identical paragraph formulas or mechanical concluding phrases.

Distinguish the strength of statements:

- **Fact:** what the original actually disclosed.
- **Derivation:** a calculable relationship with clear definitions.
- **Interpretation:** the author's causal explanation and alternatives.
- **Forecast:** why a baseline is selected, rather than a list of parameters.
- **Valuation judgment:** implications of the results, price, sensitivities, and ownership coverage.

Classify these in working papers and distinguish them naturally in prose. Do not prefix every sentence with a type label.

## Replace repeated caution with information

| Ineffective phrasing | What the reader needs |
|---|---|
| Data remain unverified, so growth cannot be inferred and uncertainty exists | Identify the missing input and whether it affects volume, price, or ownership; conclude on established parts |
| Demand growth does not necessarily benefit the company | Demand supports absorption; revenue still depends on sold volume and net prices, which become forecast drivers |
| Checks passed but do not prove the valuation | Explain dominant assumptions and reversal thresholds; keep check scope in working papers |
| Evidence supports an advantage, followed by five caveats | Explain the economic benefit and retain only qualifications material to that conclusion |

Mark cautious phrases and ask whether each adds information. Remove generic reminders; express substantive limitations through affected variables, impact, treatment, or reversal conditions. Word frequency is a diagnostic, not an automatic deletion rule. Never improve counts by deleting necessary conditions.

## Rewriting examples

Original: The operating plan is not a normalized hydrological estimate; long-run volume requires verification and cannot be directly extrapolated.

Useful rewrite: The annual plan depends on specified water conditions, so it is not extended throughout the forecast. The long-run baseline uses the recent three-year average as an evidence-limited normalization proxy. This explains the next year's forecast decline. Persistent low water would require a further revision using volume sensitivity.

Original: The baseline is RMB33.39, 17% above price, showing investment value.

Useful rewrite: Under the current operating forecast, capital costs, and ownership proxies, with the additional net claims adjustment U unresolved, the pre-adjustment baseline is RMB33.39 versus RMB28.54 at the valuation date. Operating cash generation supports value, but the difference is sensitive to capital costs: at 4% WACC the conditional value falls to RMB27.12. The evidence therefore supports the long-run operating advantage more strongly than it establishes a resilient margin of safety of approximately 17%.

These are historical case examples, not fresh investment conclusions. U does not replace treatment of the other proxy assumptions.

## Tables, language, and structure

Tables serve overviews/peer comparisons, selected historical metrics, assumptions, forecasts, equity bridges, and major sensitivities. Prose explains why; tables show how much. Do not turn every question into a matrix. Keep complete evidence in working papers.

Each table should have one purpose. Move long narrative cells into prose; allocate widths by content, align names left and numbers consistently, and state units. Charts must explain observable relationships, not decorate an unbuilt model. Cite near the fact. Concise source IDs and page numbers are useful when the index supplies titles, dates, and accessible originals.

Use direct connected reasoning. Explain technical terms on first necessary use. Translate the strength and conditions of judgments faithfully between languages. Avoid promotional claims without causal or quantitative support. Management biographies explain experience; they do not prove higher future returns.

Follow the audience and required template. The Changjiang sample uses company→industry→forecast/sensitivity→valuation→risks→disclaimer, with financial statements and sources in appendices. This is an example, not a universal six-chapter rule. Add a short opening summary when the template permits.

## Reader review

Read without working papers first: do the summary and conclusion agree; are important numbers appropriately qualified; does each section deliver a finding; does industry evidence transmit into company economics; can actuals and forecasts be distinguished; do risks connect to variables and shareholder cash; can a new reader explain the forecast and valuation?

Then challenge the conclusion with the strongest alternative explanation and evidence most likely to reverse it. Rebuild the argument where necessary instead of appending a disclaimer.
